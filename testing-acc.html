9.2 General Fourier Series and Convergence

We've been assuming \( f(x) \) has period \( 2\pi \) and defined on \( -\pi \le x < \pi \) and the resulting Fourier series is

\[ f(x) \sim \frac{1}{2} a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx) \right) \]\[ a_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) \cos(nx) \, dx \]\[ b_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) \sin(nx) \, dx \]
Standard Domain: The baseline Fourier series is defined over the interval \( [-\pi, \pi) \) with period \( T = 2\pi \). The trigonometric basis functions are \( \cos(nx) \) and \( \sin(nx) \).

Now let's first relax the period \( 2\pi \) part. Let the period be \( 2L \) (where \( L \) is the half-period) and have the function given on \( -L < x < L \).

Redefine the variable: \[ u = \frac{L}{\pi} x \] This is so when \( x = -\pi \implies u = -L \), and when \( x = \pi \implies u = L \).

Scaling Variable: The linear transformation \( u = \frac{L}{\pi} x \) (or inversely \( x = \frac{\pi}{L} u \)) stretches or compresses the canonical interval \( [-\pi, \pi] \) onto an arbitrary symmetric interval \( [-L, L] \).

Replace \( x \) with \( x = \frac{u\pi}{L} \) in the formulas above:

\[ f(u) \sim \frac{1}{2} a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left[ a_n \cos\left(n \cdot \frac{u\pi}{L}\right) + b_n \sin\left(n \cdot \frac{u\pi}{L}\right) \right] \]

\( a_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) \cos(nx) \, dx \) becomes:

\[ \begin{aligned} a_n &= \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-L}^{L} f(u) \cos\left(\frac{n\pi u}{L}\right) \cdot \frac{\pi}{L} \, du \\ &= \frac{1}{L} \int_{-L}^{L} f(u) \cos\left(\frac{n\pi u}{L}\right) \, du \end{aligned} \]

Likewise,

\[ b_n = \frac{1}{L} \int_{-L}^{L} f(u) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi u}{L}\right) \, du \]

General Fourier Series Formulation & Example

But \( u \) is just a variable, we can call it whatever we want (\( x \) or \( t \) or whatever). Now we have:

\( f(x) \) periodic with period \( 2L \), given on \( -L < x < L \):

\[ f(x) \sim \frac{1}{2} a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left[ a_n \cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) + b_n \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \right] \]\[ a_n = \frac{1}{L} \int_{-L}^{L} f(x) \cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \, dx \]\[ b_n = \frac{1}{L} \int_{-L}^{L} f(x) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \, dx \]
General Fourier Formulas: In transitioning from period \( 2\pi \) to period \( 2L \), the argument of the trigonometric basis functions changes from \( nx \) to \( \frac{n\pi x}{L} \), and the integral prefactor changes from \( \frac{1}{\pi} \) to \( \frac{1}{L} \).

Example

\[ f(t) = \begin{cases} -1 & -4 < t < 0 \\ 1 & 0 < t < 4 \end{cases} \qquad \text{Period } 8 \ (2L=8 \implies L=4 \text{ half-period}) \]

Constant Term \( a_0 \)

\[ \begin{aligned} a_0 &= \frac{1}{L} \int_{-L}^{L} f(t) \, dt \\ &= \frac{1}{4}(0) = 0 \end{aligned} \]

Net area under the curve is zero.

Cosine Coefficients \( a_n \)

\[ \begin{aligned} a_n &= \frac{1}{L} \int_{-L}^{L} f(t) \cos\left(\frac{n\pi t}{L}\right) \, dt \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \int_{-4}^{4} f(t) \cos\left(\frac{n\pi t}{4}\right) \, dt \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \left[ \int_{-4}^{0} -\cos\left(\frac{n\pi t}{4}\right) \, dt + \int_{0}^{4} \cos\left(\frac{n\pi t}{4}\right) \, dt \right] \\ &= \dots = 0 + 0 = 0 \end{aligned} \]

No cosine terms at all.

Odd Function Symmetry: Since \( f(t) \) is an odd function (\( f(-t) = -f(t) \)) and \( \cos\left(\frac{n\pi t}{4}\right) \) is an even function, their product is odd. The integral of any odd function over a symmetric interval \( [-L, L] \) is identically zero, so all \( a_n = 0 \).

Fourier Series: Sine Coefficients and Piecewise Examples

Computing Sine Coefficients \(b_n\)

For period \(2L = 8\) (where \(L = 4\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} b_n &= \frac{1}{4} \int_{-4}^{4} f(t) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi t}{L}\right) dt \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{4} \left( \int_{-4}^{0} -\sin\left(\frac{n\pi t}{4}\right) dt + \int_{0}^{4} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi t}{4}\right) dt \right) \\[6pt] &= \dots = \frac{2}{n\pi} \bigl[ 1 - \cos(n\pi) \bigr] = \frac{2}{n\pi}\bigl(1 - (-1)^n\bigr) \\[6pt] &= \begin{cases} \dfrac{4}{n\pi} & \text{if } n \text{ is odd} \\[8pt] 0 & n \text{ is even} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]

Concept Note: Since \(\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n\), the factor \(1 - (-1)^n\) evaluates to \(2\) when \(n\) is odd (since \(1 - (-1) = 2\)) and \(0\) when \(n\) is even (since \(1 - 1 = 0\)). Thus, all even harmonics vanish.

Fourier Series Expansion:

\[ \begin{aligned} f(t) &\sim \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2}{n\pi}\bigl(1 - (-1)^n\bigr) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi t}{4}\right) \\[6pt] &\sim \sum_{n \text{ odd}}^{\infty} \frac{4}{n\pi} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi t}{4}\right) \\[6pt] &\sim \frac{4}{\pi} \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right) + \frac{4}{3\pi} \sin\left(\frac{3\pi t}{4}\right) + \frac{4}{5\pi} \sin\left(\frac{5\pi t}{4}\right) + \dots \end{aligned} \]

Another Example: Sawtooth Function

Consider the piecewise function with period \(2\) (\(L = 1\)):

\[ f(t) = \begin{cases} -1 - t & -1 < t < 0 \\[4pt] 1 - t & 0 < t < 1 \end{cases}, \quad \text{period } 2 \ (L=1) \]

Fourier Cosine Coefficients \(a_0\) and \(a_n\)

\[ a_0 = \int_{-1}^{1} f(t)\, dt = 0 \quad (\text{net area is } 0) \] \[ a_n = \int_{-1}^{1} f(t) \cos\left(\frac{n\pi t}{1}\right) dt = \dots = 0 \]

Symmetry Insight: The function \(f(t)\) is an odd function (\(f(-t) = -f(t)\)). Consequently, the integral of \(f(t)\) with any symmetric interval gives zero, making \(a_0 = 0\) and all \(a_n = 0\). Only the sine terms \(b_n\) will be non-zero.

Calculation of \(b_n\) and Series Representation

\[ b_n = \int_{-1}^{1} f(t) \sin(n\pi t)\, dt = \dots = \frac{2}{n\pi} \]

Series expansion:

\[ \begin{aligned} f(t) &\sim \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2}{n\pi} \sin(n\pi t) \\[6pt] &\sim \frac{2}{\pi}\sin(\pi t) + \frac{2}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi t) + \frac{2}{3\pi}\sin(3\pi t) + \dots \end{aligned} \]

Behavior of Fourier Series at Discontinuities

Notice the common features: when there is a discontinuity, the Fourier series goes through the middle (average value).

Average value at jump discontinuity:

\[ \text{average is } \frac{1 + (-1)}{2} = 0 \implies \frac{f(t^-) + f(t^+)}{2} \]

Why? Easy to see for these two:

The first one is:

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2}{n\pi}\bigl(1 - (-1)^n\bigr) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi t}{4}\right) \]

The discontinuity is at \(t = 0\), and all \(\sin\left(\frac{n\pi t}{4}\right)\) terms are equal to \(0\). So the Fourier series has no choice but to go to \(0\).

Dirichlet's Theorem: At any jump discontinuity \(t_0\), a Fourier series converges to the arithmetic mean of its left and right limits, \(\frac{f(t_0^-) + f(t_0^+)}{2}\). For an odd square wave with values \(-1\) and \(+1\), this midpoint is precisely \(0\).

2nd Common Feature: The Overshoot (Gibbs Phenomenon)

The overshoot is always around \(9\%\) of the jump, regardless of the order (moves closer to discontinuity as \(n\) increases).

→ Harshness of “8-bit” sounds

Deep Dive: Gibbs Phenomenon & Harmonics

When a piecewise continuously differentiable function with a jump discontinuity is approximated by its Fourier partial sum \(S_N(t)\), oscillations appear near each discontinuity. The peak overshoot does not vanish as \(N \to \infty\); rather, it approaches a limit of approximately \(8.95\%\) (often rounded to \(9\%\)) of the jump height. As the order \(n\) increases, the ringing frequency increases and compresses closer to the jump point.
